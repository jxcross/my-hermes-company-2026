# Core Thesis

The current landscape of Large Language Model (LLM) hallucination mitigation is defined by a shift from single-pass generation toward multi-stage, deliberative processes. Advanced methodologies, most notably **Chain-of-Verification (CoVe)** and **Retrofitting Attribution using Research and Revision (RARR)**, seek to reduce factual errors by introducing discrete stages for verification planning and evidence-based revision.

While these iterative approaches have demonstrated significant success in boosting precision and attribution scores—transforming models from unreliable generators into precise, verifiable systems—they introduce a fundamental engineering challenge: the **accuracy-preservation trade-off**. The primary thesis of this report is that the effectiveness of hallucination mitigation must not be measured by precision alone; rather, it must be evaluated by the ability to increase information accuracy (Attributable Information) without compromising the original intent or semantic integrity (Intent Preservation) of the generated content. Success in this field will ultimately depend on optimizing the "editor" or "verification" intelligence to ensure that facts are corrected without erasing the underlying reasoning or meaning of the source text.
